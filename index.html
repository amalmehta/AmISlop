      How scores work

      Four scores, from 0 to 100. Higher is crisper. There's no total, because one number would hide which problem you have. And the easiest way to raise a single score is to write less, which isn't the same as writing better.

      These formulas and constants are invented parameters, tuned by hand on five sample messages. They aren't a validated measure of good writing. Use them to spot patterns in your own messages, not to grade other people.

      Density: is anything padding?

      Padding is words that cost the reader time and carry no meaning ("I just wanted to circle back…"). Think of it as packing peanuts: the box gets bigger, but you didn't ship more.

      • : padding words per 100 words
      • : average sentence length, in words

      In plain words: you lose 5 points for every padding word per 100, and 2 points for every word your sentences average over 22.

      Directness: does it say what it means?

      Hedges ("I think maybe") soften a point until the reader can't tell what you want. Vague words ("soon", "things") make them guess. Concrete details (numbers, dates, @names, links) earn back up to 10 points.

      • : hedges per 100 words
      • : vague words per 100 words
      • : concrete details per 100 words

      Structure: is it sturdy or taped together?

      A taped-together message jumps topics mid-paragraph ("…also the logo"), runs as a wall of text, or makes claims with no reason. A sturdy one works like a well-labelled box: one thing per compartment.

      • : topic jumps inside a paragraph (count)
      • : paragraphs over 100 words (count)
      • : share of sentences that are claims without a reason (0 to 1)

      A "claim without a reason" is a sentence with an absolute word (should, must, always, clearly) and no reason word (because, since, so that, or a number) in it or the next sentence. This is a rough heuristic: it flags some fine sentences and misses reasons phrased other ways. Ask Claude reads for meaning instead.

      Consistency: is it one voice?

      "Dear Mr. Chen… lol tbh" reads as if two people wrote it. Whichever register the message opens with sets the tone, and words from the other register are flagged.

      • : formal words (dear, kindly, pursuant to…)
      • : casual words and emoji (hey, lol, gonna, 😅…)

      The verdict

      Every score is first clamped to the range 0 to 100. Then:

      1. Crisp if every score is 75 or higher.
      2. If any score is below 50, the verdict is named after the weakest one: Padded, Hedgy or Taped together.
      3. Otherwise it's Mostly crisp, with the weakest score named.

      Under 8 words there's no verdict, because a few words can't show a pattern.

      When the rules are wrong

      The rules only see words. A message can avoid every flagged phrase and still say nothing, and a hedge can be the honest thing to write when you really are unsure. That's why Ask Claude exists: it reads for meaning, and its scores are shown next to the rule scores, not blended into them.

      The reusable idea: to measure something fuzzy, split it into parts you can count, score each part on its own, label the constants as guesses, and check them against a second, independent judge.

      Phrase lists draw on the Federal Plain Language Guidelines (plainlanguage.gov, 2011), Hyland's Hedging in Scientific Research Articles (1998), and Strunk & White's "Omit needless words".
